\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation-protocol}

The dataset contract in Section~\ref{sec:contract} defines the forecast sample. This section specifies how those samples are compared. It defines which plants are held out, what each model may observe at the forecast origin, how errors are aggregated, and how visual reliance is tested. Within each dataset track, direct comparisons use the same forecast origins, target masks, and horizon.

\subsection{Plant-disjoint partitions}

Plant identity is the unit of partitioning within each dataset track. The fixed United Kingdom assignment contains 69 training plants, 15 validation plants, and 14 test plants. Table~\ref{tab:uk-partition} gives the quality-valid power observations in each group before forecast-window and daylight filtering. Any evaluation on another track requires its own plant-disjoint partition under the same separation rule. The benchmark results section reports the UK test partition.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccl@{}}
\hline
Split & Plants & Valid steps & Use \\
\hline
Training & 69 & 846,633 & Fitting and adaptation \\
Validation & 15 & 182,809 & Early stopping and tuning \\
Test & 14 & 171,543 & Final evaluation \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{United Kingdom plant partition after quality-controlled exclusions. Each installation belongs to exactly one partition.}
\label{tab:uk-partition}
\end{table}

The evaluator advances by one forecast horizon between origins and applies the target-validity and daylight masks to the windows defined in Section~\ref{sec:contract}. For the United Kingdom partition, this leaves 19,964 test windows and 165,295 scored target steps. The 171,543 test steps in Table~\ref{tab:uk-partition} count valid power observations before those filters.

Forecast origins denote the timestamps separating historical observations from future horizon steps. Eligible evaluation points are governed by two filters: a target-validity mask excluding sensor faults and missing intervals, and a daylight mask restricting evaluation to solar elevation angles above zero. Evaluating all models across identical masked steps guarantees that performance differences reflect true forecasting quality under uniform test periods.

Training plants supply fitted parameters, including adapter weights, and any retrieval datastore. Validation plants determine checkpoints and hyperparameters, including retrieval weights when tuned. Test plants are used only for final reporting. This boundary also applies to learned normalization and feature selection. Each implementation declares its fitted statistics. The loader checks that plant assignments do not overlap.

\subsection{Scenarios and input parity}

The primary benchmark is the United Kingdom plant-disjoint comparison. The cross-region experiment applies models trained on UK plants to all eight eligible US plants. Checkpoints from seeds 42, 43, and 44 remain fixed during US inference, and US power is resampled to 30-minute cadence. Thus, the two reported settings measure transfer to unseen plants within the UK and transfer from the UK to the US. Across these comparisons, each model receives the modalities its design supports. Persistence forecasts use no satellite frames. Numerical forecasters use power history and, where supported, covariates. Multimodal systems can also use recent imagery. \subsection{Baseline implementations and comparison boundaries}
\label{sec:baseline-implementations}

The models reviewed in Chapter~\ref{cha:related} are compared under the track-specific plant split and input rules above. This subsection specifies each model’s inputs, whether it learns from the training plants, and whether its reported score uses the same evaluation set as other methods.

The non-learned references use only permitted observations. Persistence repeats the latest valid power value across the horizon. Seasonal naive reuses the value from the same clock time on the previous day. Hourly climatology estimates a plant's typical output at each hour from its available history. Smart Persistence carries the latest observed clearness relationship forward as the deterministic solar envelope changes~\cite{haurwitz}. It supplies the denominator of the skill score.

Tabular models (LightGBM~\cite{lightgbm} and TabPFN~\cite{tabpfn3}) operate on fixed feature tables of lagged power, calendar variables, and weather observations without satellite frames or future covariates. Supervised sequence baselines (PatchTST~\cite{patchtst}, TFT~\cite{tft}, DLinear~\cite{dlinear}, and an MLP baseline) train on the same plant partition using historical covariates. iTransformer~\cite{itransformer} models cross-variable interactions directly, providing a non-visual multi-variable reference.

The time-series foundation-model entries start from released checkpoints. Chronos-2~\cite{chronos2} is evaluated both zero-shot and after adaptation on training plants as the numerical control of the fusion ladder. TimesFM~\cite{timesfm} and TiRex~\cite{tirex} are used zero-shot. Tiny Time Mixer~\cite{ttm} appears in zero-shot and fine-tuned forms. CoRA~\cite{cora} adds a covariate adapter to a mostly frozen backbone. TS-RAG~\cite{tsrag} and Cross-RAG~\cite{crossrag} condition the forecaster on windows retrieved from training plants. These entries receive no imagery. Their architectures, pretraining corpora, and adaptation strategies differ. Their rankings therefore compare benchmark performance across methods and do not constitute an architectural ablation.

Multimodal baselines encompass both satellite-conditioned models (MMTSFM, Aurora, Solar-VLM, SUNSET) and visual time-series rendering methods (Time-VLM, UniCast). All models are scored across the held-out test installations on valid daylight steps.

\subsection{Scoring and reporting}

Within each track, valid daylight targets are scored separately for every held-out plant before the plant scores are averaged. This gives installations equal weight even when their usable records differ in length. The two co-primary measures answer different questions. Skill score measures the reduction in NRMSE relative to Smart Persistence across the scored targets. Ramp NMAE measures error during rapid changes in observed power. A gain in overall skill therefore does not compensate for worse ramp accuracy. The formulas are given in Section~\ref{sec:metric-definitions}.

Models that produce quantiles also report the CRPS approximation, 80\% interval coverage, and quantile expected calibration error. These metrics use the same valid-daylight mask and plant weighting.

Training variation is reported alongside these scores. Learned methods use fixed random seeds for data ordering and parameter initialization.

For multimodal models, a score advantage alone does not show that the trained forecast uses images. Each trained checkpoint is therefore evaluated with vision active and again with its visual embeddings set to zero. The numerical embeddings and token positions remain in place. Both passes use the same weights, forecast origins, target masks, ramp thresholds, and aggregation. Their differences in normalized error and ramp NMAE measure sensitivity to visual input under that checkpoint. This comparison keeps the training route, capacity, and regularization fixed. A separately trained numerical model would change those factors as well. A negligible vision-on/off difference can therefore coexist with a nominal advantage over a numerical model. Higher error with vision disabled indicates a positive contribution from the visual pathway under this checkpoint and intervention.

The vision-on/off pass cannot identify which part of the clip matters. The cached-latent-order, stale-image, and wrong-plant-image substitution interventions are specified in Section~\ref{cha:ablations}. They use the trained weights and, where possible, the same forecast origins. They are reported apart from standard model comparisons because they change the input to an existing model. A wrong-plant-image substitution needs a clip from a different test plant at the same time. Shuffled test batches allow the evaluator to find such a donor when series-major batches would contain only one plant.

Each reported result identifies the dataset track and plant partition, physical window lengths, input modalities, exact covariate fields, and forecast-origin rules. It also identifies the random seeds, numbers of windows and scored target steps, checkpoint-selection metric, tuning budget, and whether the comparison was prespecified or exploratory.

Multi-anchor achieves the highest mean skill score at 10.46 percent and the lowest overall NMAE at 0.1657, improving on the numerical control at 8.90 percent skill and 0.1719 NMAE. It exceeds spatial-token fusion in skill across all three paired seeds. On ramp intervals, both structured visual architectures improve over the numerical baseline. Spatial-token fusion records the lowest ramp NMAE at 0.1692, followed closely by multi-anchor at 0.1702, compared with 0.1864 for the numerical control. The localized cloud information in spatial tokens and multi-temporal anchors helps the network anticipate steep power swings under unfamiliar US atmospheric conditions.

In contrast, pooled-image fusion yields negative skill at $-3.03$ percent and higher errors across the board, with an NMAE of 0.1933 and a ramp NMAE of 0.2008. By retaining spatial layout, spatial-token fusion matches the numerical control on overall skill ($8.85$ percent versus $8.90$ percent) while achieving lower ramp error. Multi-anchor fusion gains further by distributing tokens across five observation times, providing temporal progression across the context window.

Its single-seed run attains 3.46 percent skill and 0.1786 ramp NMAE, tracking ramps better than the numerical baseline (0.1864) and pooled-image fusion (0.2008). Across the main arms, calibration reflects the regional transfer. Mean nominal 80 percent coverage ranges from 0.409 for spatial-token fusion to 0.523 for pooled-image fusion, while multi-anchor reaches 0.459, lower than the UK coverage levels. These eight plants provide evidence across different climatic zones, though they represent a quality-filtered subset observed over a single evaluation period.

\section{Results and ablations}
\label{cha:ablations}

The benchmark in Section~\ref{cha:results} establishes the forecasting performance of the proposed models. The ablations examine what supports that performance: whether the fitted models use their visual inputs, which properties of those inputs matter, and how the fusion interface affects transfer to unseen plants. Unless stated otherwise, results use the UK cross-plant test, the self-attention temporal mixer, and seeds 42, 43, and 44. Interpretation follows the size and direction of the observed changes, their consistency across matched seeds, and the variables changed by each intervention. Small changes are informative when they narrow the explanation of a larger gain; losses reveal where an additional design choice fails to improve the tested configuration.

The main result is that spatial-token fusion improves point forecasts across most held-out plants and depends on timely, site-matched imagery. The controls refine this finding. Fractional token placement has little observed effect, mean pooling improves skill while retaining spatially distinct cells, and freezing the numerical encoder largely preserves point performance at a calibration cost. The multi-anchor experiments extend the same visual interface to older observations and expose a trade-off between temporal coverage and the payload available at each anchor. Together, these comparisons provide an empirical account of the fusion design, with the scope of each conclusion tied to the intervention that produced it.

\subsection{Interface and content controls}

The anchor-placement check asks how much the forecast depends on the precise positions assigned to visual tokens. Moving the same 98 tokens to one shared integer position changes mean skill by $-0.02$ percentage points and ramp NMAE by $+0.0004$ across three paired seeds. Both differences change direction across seeds. The fitted interface therefore tolerates this positional simplification with little observed change in point performance. This narrows the explanation of the spatial-token gain: fractional placement is not supported as its main source. The check preserves the visual payload and tests one placement change, so its conclusion concerns positional precision within this interface.

Under paired-novelty selection, which retains paired endpoints of moving trajectories instead of individual dynamic cells, multi-anchor skill reaches 54.16 percent, a 0.07 percentage point increase over the default selector. Because changing the trajectory selection rule leaves the gap to spatial-token fusion (55.10 percent) largely unchanged, the multi-anchor shortfall is primarily associated with the reduced per-anchor token budget (20 tokens versus 98) and the dilution of visual capacity across multiple days, rather than the specific token ranking heuristic.

Image timing produces a much larger response. Shifting the clip six hours into the past raises ramp NMAE by between 0.0156 and 0.0182 across three seeds and lowers skill by about 13.5 percentage points. The stale clip performs worse than disabling vision. Supplying an image can therefore hurt the fitted model when that image describes an earlier state. The consistent degradation shows that the model's use of vision depends on temporal relevance and supports matching the clip to the forecast origin.

Spatial correspondence matters as well. Substituting another plant's contemporaneous clip raises ramp NMAE by between 0.0163 and 0.0189 across three seeds and lowers skill from about 55 to 37 percent. Every seed degrades. Contemporaneous imagery from elsewhere cannot substitute for the site's own clip in the fitted model. Together, the timing and wrong-plant controls support content grounding: predictions respond to whether the supplied visual evidence matches the place and time being forecast. These substitutions identify the importance of that correspondence without identifying the individual visual features responsible.

At the fixed 98-token budget, random selection raises ramp NMAE by 0.0039 and lowers skill by 1.52 percentage points across two paired seeds. Vision-off scores stay unchanged. Holding the count fixed makes the selected payload relevant to the result: equal-length visual inputs do not give equal performance. Novelty-selected tokens better serve the model trained with that selector. Because the intervention changes selection only at inference, the observed loss includes any mismatch with the training distribution; it supports retaining the trained selection rule without establishing its superiority under separate retraining.

Permuting V-JEPA's cached temporal latents after visual encoding changes ramp NMAE by no more than 0.00030 in any seed. The small response localizes the observed sensitivity: the downstream forecast depends little on this ordering of the already encoded latents. V-JEPA may have mixed motion information within each latent before the permutation, so this result concerns the fusion stage's access to latent order. Frame order before encoding remains unchanged. The check helps distinguish reliance on the encoded visual content from reliance on its explicit downstream temporal arrangement.

The token-budget sweep shows that increasing the available visual payload does not automatically improve a fitted model. Evaluating without pruning to the 98-token budget raises ramp NMAE by 0.0102 across three seeds. Keep settings of 0.3 and 0.7 raise it by 0.0022 and 0.0069. Error is lowest near the count used during training. These results support preserving the trained token budget at inference and show that the interface is sensitive to sequence length. Since the alternative settings are evaluated without retraining, 98 tokens is the best tested inference setting for these weights, with no claim of a universal budget optimum.

The spatial-resolution probe finds higher ramp $R^2$ with a mean-pooled 4$\times$4 grid than with 7$\times$7 at 30, 60, and 120 minutes. A coarser representation can therefore retain useful predictive information and outperform the finer grid under this probe. This result weakens the assumption that additional spatial cells necessarily improve the representation and motivates considering compression as a design variable. Both probe grids use mean pooling, whereas the deployed arm uses pixel-shuffle concatenation, so the comparison ranks the probe representations and leaves the corresponding end-to-end grid choice unresolved.

The end-to-end pooling check provides a more direct test of sub-patch detail. With the number and positions of selected cells fixed, replacing pixel-shuffle concatenation with mean pooling raises three-seed skill from 55.10 to 55.64 percent. The skill gain appears in every paired seed. Ramp NMAE falls from 0.1440 to 0.1435, and 80 percent coverage rises from 0.731 to 0.753. The larger change in skill and smaller change in ramp error show that the representation choice affects the reported metrics to different degrees. Spatially distinct cells remain available under mean pooling, so the result supports a compressed spatial payload and revises the motivation for preserving sub-patch detail. The original spatial-token gain does not require that detail in this comparison.

Freezing the adapted numerical encoder's blocks during visual-arm training yields three-seed skill of 55.29 percent and ramp NMAE of 0.1441, close to spatial-token fusion's 55.10 percent and 0.1440. Point forecasting therefore remains effective when visual training leaves those blocks fixed. The uncertainty measures reveal a separate trade-off: coverage falls from 0.731 to 0.710, while quantile ECE rises from 0.0359 to 0.0444. Allowing encoder adaptation has more apparent value for calibration than for the point scores in this check. The intervention changes the trainability of the blocks together and does not attribute the calibration difference to a particular block.

The vision-off columns test image use within each fitted model by holding its weights fixed. Disabling vision in pooled-image fusion raises all-daylight NMAE by 0.0008, while the mean ramp change rounds to 0.0000 and has no consistent direction across the three seeds. This comparison jointly changes anchor count, daily spacing, latent depth, and per-anchor token budget. It measures the consequence of that combined design and cannot assign the gap to older observations alone.

The full payload feasibility run retains four latent slices and 98 tokens at each of five anchors. It reaches 54.49 percent skill and 0.1458 ramp NMAE, with vision-off increases of 0.0040 and 0.0041. This run demonstrates that a larger multi-anchor payload can be trained and used by the forecast. Its 533-position sequence is substantially longer than the 141-position single-anchor interface. One completed seed and a smaller eligible target set make its point-score ranking descriptive. The experiment establishes feasibility and visual reliance for this larger interface; the recorded results do not establish that restoring payload closes the gap to spatial-token fusion.

The matched seeds provide a check on whether the configuration rankings depend on a particular numerical warm start. Visual arms with the same seed begin from the same adapted numerical checkpoint, although their subsequent training trajectories differ. Point metrics show limited variation within each arm across the three recorded runs.

Spatial-token fusion improves both skill and ramp NMAE over the numerical baseline in every paired seed. Multi-anchor has lower skill and higher ramp NMAE than spatial-token fusion in all three pairs. These rankings persist across the recorded initializations and agree with the per-plant and lead-time patterns. Three runs give a bounded view of training variability, but the agreement supports the observed configuration preference. Explaining the multi-anchor loss still requires separating anchor count, temporal depth, token allocation, and daily spacing.

The probabilistic measures assess different properties. CRPS summarizes quantile forecast error across the predicted distribution. Coverage counts how often observations fall between the predicted 10th and 90th percentiles; that interval should contain about 80 percent of observations. Quantile ECE measures departures between each quantile's stated probability and its observed frequency.

Calibration varies more clearly across the recorded configurations. Spatial-token coverage is below the nominal 80 percent in every seed. Its quantile ECE exceeds the numerical baseline's in every seed. Multi-anchor coverage is also below nominal in all three runs. Its ECE is higher than spatial-token fusion's in each matched seed. Spatial-token fusion has lower CRPS than the numerical baseline in each recorded run, with three-seed means of 0.0544 and 0.0574, respectively.

The lower CRPS shows that spatial-token fusion improves the reported distributional error as well as the point scores, while coverage and ECE identify a calibration cost. These measures capture different properties of the forecast and should inform different modeling decisions. The freezing control is relevant here: it leaves point scores close to the original arm while worsening calibration further. Interval widths and individual quantile predictions are unavailable for this analysis, so the undercoverage cannot be attributed specifically to narrow intervals, displaced intervals, or a combination of the two.

The full payload seed has higher skill and coverage than multi-anchor seed 42, with identical ramp NMAE when rounded to four decimals. It also scores fewer forecast targets. This is evidence from one larger multi-anchor run, so the apparent improvement cannot establish a repeatable payload effect. Pooled-image fusion illustrates a different form of run variation: coverage is below nominal in two seeds and slightly above it in the third, while point scores remain near the numerical baseline.

Taken together, the ablations support spatial-token fusion as the strongest tested main configuration for UK cross-plant point forecasting. Its improvement is accompanied by image reliance, sensitivity to timing and site correspondence, and gains distributed across held-out plants and forecast leads. The smaller-effect controls refine the design argument: precise fractional placement contributes little in the tested intervention, mean pooling preserves the useful spatial interface while improving skill, and encoder adaptation has a clearer benefit for calibration than for point prediction. The multi-anchor results document useful visual forecasts with a longer history, alongside a consistent UK point-score loss relative to the recent single-anchor arm. These findings establish which configurations worked and which simplifications remained effective, while keeping the causal interpretation at the level resolved by the completed experiments.
